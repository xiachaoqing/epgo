Five English Grammar Patterns A2–B1 Learners Can Practise Today

Grammar practice works best when a learner can notice a pattern, use it in a meaningful sentence and correct one mistake. These five patterns appear in everyday English and in many KET and PET tasks. Practise them in context instead of memorising isolated rules.

Study method: read the examples, cover them, write your own sentence and then check the form. A correct answer you can explain is more valuable than a guess.

1. Past simple for finished events

Use the past simple for an action that finished at a known time: “We visited the museum on Saturday.” Check irregular verbs separately because their forms cannot be predicted. Add a time phrase to make the meaning clear, then change the subject: “My brother visited the museum.”

2. Present perfect for experience

Use have or has plus the past participle when the exact time is not important: “I have seen that film.” Compare it with the past simple: “I saw that film last night.” If you write a finished time such as last night, use the past simple.

3. Comparatives for useful differences

Use “-er” or “more” to compare two things: “This route is shorter,” or “The second story is more interesting.” Add than when you name the second item. Practise one adjective in three forms: short, shorter, the shortest.

4. First conditional for real possibilities

Use if plus the present simple, followed by will plus the base verb: “If it rains, we will stay inside.” The order can change: “We will stay inside if it rains.” Avoid putting will directly after if in this pattern.

5. Relative clauses for clearer sentences

Use who for people and which or that for things: “The teacher who helped me was patient.” A relative clause joins two short ideas and avoids repeating a noun. Start with one sentence about a person or object you know.

A five-minute self-check

  1. Choose one pattern and write three sentences.
  2. Underline the verb or connector that carries the pattern.
  3. Change the subject or time phrase.
  4. Read the sentences aloud and correct one error.

Keep a record of the pattern that caused trouble. Revisit it after one day, two days and four days. Spaced review makes a small amount of grammar practice more durable.
