How to Review English Vocabulary Without Forgetting It

Vocabulary becomes useful when you can recognise a word, remember its meaning and use it in a new sentence. Copying a long list may feel productive, but it gives the brain few chances to retrieve meaning. A short review cycle with personal examples is easier to repeat and easier to measure.

Keep the list small: five to eight words from one story or lesson are enough for one review cycle.

Step 1: record a meaningful example

Write the word, a simple meaning and one sentence connected to your life. For “borrow,” a learner might write “I borrow a book from the library on Saturday.” The sentence gives the word a situation, grammar pattern and memory cue.

Step 2: retrieve before looking

Cover the meaning and example. Say or write what you remember. Only then check the answer. Retrieval feels slower than rereading, but it shows what is available without help. Mark each word with a tick, question mark or cross so the next session has a clear focus.

Step 3: change the sentence

On the next review, keep the meaning but change the subject, tense or situation. “I borrowed a book” can become “My sister borrowed my headphones.” This small change proves that the learner understands the word rather than memorising one sentence.

A four-session cycle

  1. Session 1: learn five to eight words and create examples.
  2. Session 2, one day later: retrieve meanings and correct the difficult words.
  3. Session 3, two days later: write new sentences and read them aloud.
  4. Session 4, four days later: use the words in a short message or story.

Choose words by usefulness

Prioritise words that appear in the learner’s current stories, lessons and conversations. A word that can be used in several sentences is often more valuable than a rare word that appears once. Grouping by a real topic can help, but avoid memorising a translation-only list without examples.
